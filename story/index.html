My story

My name carries a vineyard in it. I didn't know that for most of my life — and then it explained everything my family had already lived. This is the long version: a ranch outside Carlsbad, the house my father built, the vine that grew in our yard, and the kid who had to learn to walk twice.

El nombre

Parra is Spanish for the grapevine you train over a patio — not the wild vine, the cultivated one. The one somebody planted, tended, and shaped for years so that other people could sit in its shade. In 1597, the Spanish crown granted land in a desert valley of Coahuila so green with wild vines they named it Santa María de las Parras — Saint Mary of the Grapevines. The winery founded there is the oldest in the Americas, and it is still pouring.

Then the vines went north. Up El Camino Real de Tierra Adentro, in the hands of missionaries, until they were planted along the Rio Grande in 1629 — which is why New Mexico, not California, is the oldest wine country in the United States. The road those vines traveled runs through Albuquerque, where I live and work.

Cultivated. Rooted. Carried north. I didn't choose the metaphor — it was in the name the whole time. And, as you're about to see, it was in the backyard too.

El rancho

My father, Rodolfo Ceballos Parra, was born in Delicias, Chihuahua, and grew up the way ranch kids do — working beside his brothers from the time he could carry anything. At nineteen he came north to Carlsbad, New Mexico, where his older brother Angel was already working a ranch: cotton fields, cattle, chickens, pigs — the whole catalogue of before-dawn work.

The ranch was run by the Gonzalez family — Santiago, his wife Natalia, and their seven children. Santiago had started from deep in Mexico, in Jalisco. He crossed first, in 1945, as one of los braceros — the generation of Mexican men the United States called north to work its fields when it needed their hands — and then he did the patient work of a lifetime: his family in Juárez for years while he earned the whole distance, until he had carried every one of them over that line. He came to run the very ranch in Carlsbad where young men from Chihuahua — my father among them — would get their footing in America, and he lived to be one hundred years old.

My mother was one of those seven children — born in Juárez, living on the ranch by the time she was five. And the young man from Delicias who came to work her father's fields fell in love with her. The man running the ranch became my grandfather. Parras climb; that's what they're for.

La casa que construyó mi padre

My parents built their life out of trades and early alarms. My father became a welder for oil-and-gas companies; my mother has been a CNA for forty-five years and counting, now a restorative aide — four decades of waking before the sun to take care of other people's families. Nobody handed them anything. They made the American dream the way you make anything that lasts: by hand, every day.

My father built our house himself. And in the yard — I am not inventing this — he grew a parra. A real one: a big, beautiful grapevine, heavy and arched, in its prime for years before it finally dried out. Apple trees. Pecan trees. More roses than any welder will admit to planting. My sister AnaIsabel came first; fifteen years later, me — the kid who got to grow up in the shade of all of it.

We are Catholic, and we hold it strongly — faith was load-bearing in that house. And ours is the open kind: the kind that believes grace is real and that the shade of the vine is for everyone who comes to sit under it.

Education was the house's other religion. A welder and a caregiver raised a biochemist and a technologist — my sister went into the sciences of life, I went into the machines — and we were both brought up on the same creed: knowledge is power, and you earn it like everything else in this family, early and daily.

Their values came with instructions, and I carry them word for word. Work hard, and take care of the things you love. Respect everyone — and if the moment ever truly calls for it, slap with a white glove and move with grace. Speak with action, not with words. I have never found better engineering principles.

El milagro

I arrived with complications. A clubbed right foot. Kidneys that didn't cooperate. At one point the delivery room called a Code Blue. I spent most of my infancy in leg braces and learned to walk late. My family calls me the miracle baby, and I've stopped arguing with them.

I started school in Spanish. Kindergarten through second grade is where English happened to me — with my mother, fluent in both worlds, translating the one into the other. Every first-generation kid knows exactly the job I'm describing.

In second grade my mother noticed I was walking strangely. Dr. Baca in Carlsbad — the orthopedist who had treated my clubfoot as a newborn, back when I was one of the first patients of his career — found Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease, a rare hip condition that mostly comes for young boys. Hip surgery in third grade. Foot surgery in fourth. That fourth-grade year I was homeschooled, spent most of it sitting, and learned to walk for the second time in my life.

Those years were waiting rooms — countless appointments, x-rays, follow-ups — and the waiting rooms were my first real education. I was surrounded by kids fighting far worse than I was: kids for whom the simple tasks — standing, reaching, crossing a room — weren't simple at all. I understood two things at once, and young: that people saw me differently, and that I was one of the lucky ones. Both of those stayed.

Middle school and high school, I worked at being a normal kid in a body with a no-running clause — no jumping, no impact. So I got good at the patient things instead: watching, reading, building, fixing. Looking back, that was the training.

And the truth is, this part of the story doesn't close neatly. The hips still ache today, and someday both will need replacing. The pain is there; so am I. God has allowed me to come this far on the hips He blessed me with, and I intend to take them as far as they will go.

El camino

Then Albuquerque. In my family the road has only ever bent north — Jalisco to Juárez, Delicias to Carlsbad, Carlsbad up the Rio Grande to Albuquerque, the same direction the vines traveled four hundred years ago. I took the non-traditional route into tech: a community college degree, a bootcamp, and every door I was willing to knock on.

La cosecha

These days I work in energy, and I've come to think of it as harvest work. At GridFlow I help build long-duration batteries — machines that hold a day's sun for the hours after dark. At Pii Energy I write the software that puts that kind of power into ordinary homes. Along the way there was research, too: one summer spent studying the Sun itself, another turning molten glass into science. The details — the instruments, the posters, the products — live on my work and my research pages.

Because the mission is simpler than the résumé. My family spent generations pulling a living out of dry ground — cotton, cattle, a grapevine coaxed out of the desert. I do the same work with different tools: I harvest the sun, store it, and hand it to people so the lights stay on. I never really left the ranch. We just changed the crop.

La mesa larga

A parra exists so other people can sit under it. That's the part of the name I take most seriously. I serve on the board of NM Tech Talks, direct the New Mexico chapter of Techqueria for Latinos in tech, and founded Q-STEM, a meetup for LGBTQ+ folks in STEM — because the table New Mexico tech sits at should be long enough for everyone who wants a seat.

People ask why the site says PARRA like a fashion house. It isn't vanity — it's inheritance. A grandfather who carried a family across a border one patient year at a time. A welder who built a house and planted a grapevine in the desert. A mother who has spent forty-five years caring for people. And a kid in leg braces who learned to walk twice and decided that was just the first lesson. I'm not the traditional path — I'm the trained vine. Paths aren't linear. Neither are parras.

Thanks for reading this far. Come sit under the vine.
