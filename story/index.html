My story

My name carries a vineyard in it. I didn't know that for most of my life — and then it explained everything my family had already lived. This is my story — the ranch, the house my father built, the vine in our yard, and the people who carried me.

El nombre

Parra is Spanish for the grapevine you train over a patio — not the wild vine, the cultivated one. The one somebody plants, tends, and shapes for years so that other people can sit in its shade. The name carries four centuries: a 1597 land grant in a Coahuila valley so green with vines they called it Santa María de las Parras, home of the oldest winery in the Americas — and the vines that traveled north up El Camino Real to the Rio Grande in 1629, which is why New Mexico, not California, is the oldest wine country in the United States.

But I didn't learn any of that from a book. I learned it from a yard in Carlsbad, where my father — a welder who built our house with his own hands — grew a parra of his own: big, beautiful, heavy-armed, in its prime for years before it finally dried out. Apple trees. Pecan trees. More roses than any welder will admit to planting. I grew up in the shade of the thing my name means. I didn't choose the metaphor; I was raised under it.

Las raíces

I exist because a ranch outside Carlsbad needed hands. My father, Rodolfo, came up from Delicias, Chihuahua at nineteen to work it — cotton fields, cattle, the whole catalogue of before-dawn work — following his older brother, who was already there. The ranch was run by Santiago Gonzalez: a man who had crossed in 1945 as one of los braceros, then done the patient work of a lifetime — his wife Natalia and their seven children waiting years in Juárez while he earned the whole distance, until he had carried every one of them over that line. He lived to be one hundred years old.

One of those seven children was my mother, on the ranch by the time she was five. The young man from Delicias fell in love with the ranch manager's daughter — and the man who ran the ranch became my grandfather. Parras climb; that's what they're for.

My parents turned that inheritance into a life of trades and early alarms: my father welding for the oil fields, my mother a CNA for forty-five years and counting, now a restorative aide — four decades of waking before the sun to care for other people's families. Nobody handed them anything; they made the American dream the way you make anything that lasts, by hand, every day. And they raised a biochemist and a technologist — my sister went into the sciences of life, I went into the machines — on one creed: knowledge is power, and you earn it early and daily.

We are Catholic, and we hold it strongly — faith was load-bearing in our house, and ours is the open kind: the kind that believes grace is real and the shade of the vine is for everyone who comes to sit under it. The rest of the code came with instructions, and I carry them word for word: work hard, and take care of the things you love; respect everyone — and if the moment ever truly calls for it, slap with a white glove and move with grace; speak with action, not with words. I have never found better engineering principles.

El milagro

I came along fifteen years after my sister, and I arrived with complications. A clubbed right foot. Kidneys that didn't cooperate. At one point the delivery room called a Code Blue. I spent most of my infancy in leg braces and learned to walk late. My family calls me the miracle baby, and I've stopped arguing with them.

I started school in Spanish. Kindergarten through second grade is where English happened to me — with my mother, fluent in both worlds, translating the one into the other. Every first-generation kid knows exactly the job I'm describing.

In second grade my mother noticed I was walking strangely. Dr. Baca in Carlsbad — the orthopedist who had treated my clubfoot as a newborn, back when I was one of the first patients of his career — found Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease, a rare hip condition that mostly comes for young boys. So the braces came back — second grade through fourth — along with hip surgery in third grade and foot surgery in fourth. That fourth-grade year I was homeschooled, spent most of it sitting, and had to learn to walk all over again.

Those years were waiting rooms — countless appointments, x-rays, follow-ups — and the waiting rooms were my first real education. I was surrounded by kids fighting far worse than I was: kids for whom the simple tasks — standing, reaching, crossing a room — weren't simple at all. I understood two things at once, and young: that people saw me differently, and that I was one of the lucky ones. Both of those stayed.

The lessons got concrete early — literally. My elementary school had no ramp at the front door, a detail nobody notices until the kid in leg braces is yours. My mother did what mothers do, and she did it the family way: stern, graceful, white glove. The ramp was installed within weeks. It's still there — I see it every time I visit home — and it holds more than concrete should: the fight it takes to make a place accessible, the children who were waiting on it, and a mother who went to every length so her son could get to school a little easier. Strength looks like a lot of things. Sometimes it's a ramp.

Middle school and high school, I worked at being a normal kid in a body with a no-running clause — no jumping, no impact. So I got good at the patient things instead: watching, reading, building, fixing. Looking back, that was the training.

And the truth is, this part of the story doesn't close neatly. The hips still ache today, and someday both will need replacing. The pain is there; so am I. God has allowed me to come this far on the hips He blessed me with, and I intend to take them as far as they will go.

El camino

Then Albuquerque. In my family the road has only ever bent north — Jalisco to Juárez, Delicias to Carlsbad, Carlsbad up the Rio Grande to Albuquerque, the same direction the vines traveled four hundred years ago. I took the non-traditional route into tech: a community college degree, a bootcamp, and every door I was willing to knock on.

La cosecha

These days I work in energy, and I've come to think of it as harvest work. At GridFlow I help build long-duration batteries — machines that hold a day's sun for the hours after dark. At Pii Energy I write the software that puts that kind of power into ordinary homes. Along the way there was research, too: one summer spent studying the Sun itself, another turning molten glass into science. The details — the instruments, the posters, the products — live on my work and my research pages.

Because the mission is simpler than the résumé. My family spent generations pulling a living out of dry ground — cotton, cattle, a grapevine coaxed out of the desert. I do the same work with different tools: I harvest the sun, store it, and hand it to people so the lights stay on. I never really left the ranch. We just changed the crop.

La mesa larga

A parra exists so other people can sit under it. That's the part of the name I take most seriously. I serve on the board of NM Tech Talks, direct the New Mexico chapter of Techqueria for Latinos in tech, and founded Q-STEM, a meetup for LGBTQ+ folks in STEM — because the table New Mexico tech sits at should be long enough for everyone who wants a seat.

The name on this site isn't branding — it's inheritance. A grandfather who carried a family across a border one patient year at a time. A welder who built a house and planted a grapevine in the desert. A mother who has spent forty-five years caring for people. And a kid in leg braces who got back up and decided that was just the first lesson. I'm not the traditional path — I'm the trained vine. Paths aren't linear. Neither are parras.

Thanks for reading this far. Come sit under the vine.
