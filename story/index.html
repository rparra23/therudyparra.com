My story

My name carries a vineyard in it. I didn't know that for most of my life — and then it explained everything I was already doing.

El nombre

Parra is Spanish for the grapevine you train over a patio — not the wild vine, the cultivated one. The one somebody planted, tended, and shaped for years so that other people could sit in its shade. In 1597, the Spanish crown granted land in a desert valley of Coahuila so green with wild vines they named it Santa María de las Parras — Saint Mary of the Grapevines. The winery founded there is the oldest in the Americas, and it is still pouring.

Then the vines went north. Up El Camino Real de Tierra Adentro, in the hands of missionaries, until they were planted along the Rio Grande in 1629 — which is why New Mexico, not California, is the oldest wine country in the United States. The road those vines traveled runs through Albuquerque, where I live and work.

Cultivated. Rooted. Carried north. I didn't choose the metaphor — it was in the name the whole time.

Two summers of science

In the summer of 2020 — peak COVID — I did solar physics from a bedroom in southeast New Mexico. The National Solar Observatory and CU Boulder ran their research program fully remote that year: a cohort scattered from the Midwest to Ireland to Dubai, photoshopped together against the Flatirons because none of us ever stood in the same room. I spent that summer as sole first author studying how the H-alpha line breathes over the Sun's 27-day rotation, and found that sunspots — not faculae, not filaments — do most of the talking. The work was published under NASA and NSF sponsorship. It's all on my research page, poster and all.

Three summers later I was in Cedar Rapids, Iowa, leading a team in the Feller Lab at Coe College — melting sodium borate glass at 1300 °C, pouring it into roller quenchers, and reading its atomic structure with X-ray absorption spectroscopy. Two more published posters came out of that summer, and a habit I kept: writing the procedure down so carefully that the next person can't get it wrong.

The bench

I'm a lab technician at GridFlow, a clean-energy startup building vanadium redox flow batteries for long-duration commercial storage — the kind of batteries that hold a day's sun for the hours after sunset. I run the R&D lab end-to-end: prototypes, custom electronics, gloveboxes, the safety protocols, and the standard operating procedures for a machine that has never existed before.

I'm also the lead software developer at Pii Energy, GridFlow's consumer line, where I built the full-stack platform behind the Edge 2000 — a plug-in solar hybrid system that launched on Pi Day 2025 and was featured in PV Magazine. Before that: telemetry firmware for industrial well controllers, and OS image work at Lockheed Martin. The throughline has never changed — I build things that bridge electrons, atoms, and code.

La mesa larga

A parra exists so other people can sit under it. That's the part of the name I take most seriously. I serve on the board of NM Tech Talks, direct the New Mexico chapter of Techqueria for Latinos in tech, and founded Q-STEM, a meetup for LGBTQ+ folks in STEM — because the table New Mexico tech sits at should be long enough for everyone who wants a seat.

That's the brand, and it was never really a brand. It's a name that was already doing the work: something planted carefully, tended daily, and grown for shade that other people get to enjoy.

Thanks for reading this far. Come sit under the vine.
